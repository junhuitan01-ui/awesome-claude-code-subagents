# Project brief

## Project name
major-project-ideas

## Purpose
An example run of the research system. The report gives a student practical, evidence-backed "additional ideas" they could add to a major project (a capstone, final-year or senior design project) to make it stronger, and shows how to choose among them without overloading the project.

## Audience
University or polytechnic students planning or midway through a major project, mainly in computing and engineering, but the advice should transfer to other fields. Readers are not experts in project management. They care about a better grade, a portfolio piece they can show employers, and finishing on time.

## Research questions
1. What do capstone and final-year project guides and assessors value, and which of those qualities do students often under-deliver?
2. What kinds of additional ideas or features are commonly recommended to strengthen a major project (for example user testing, accessibility, automated testing, security, documentation, data or evaluation, ethics, sustainability, deployment)? Give concrete examples.
3. How should a student choose, prioritise and scope additions (for example must-have vs stretch goals, time and risk)?
4. What are the risks or downsides of adding extra ideas, and how can they be managed?

## Scope
- **In scope:** generic advice for any major project, with computing and engineering examples; published university guidance, rubrics, standards bodies and established practice.
- **Out of scope:** a specific student's project; ideas lists with no reasoning; paid writing services.
- **Time period / geography:** current practice (roughly 2015 onward); any country, English-language sources.

## Sources
- **Preferred or required sources:** university capstone or final-year project handbooks and marking rubrics, accreditation or professional bodies (e.g. ABET, ACM, IEEE, Engineers Australia, W3C), peer-reviewed education research.
- **Sources to avoid:** content farms, essay-mill sites, "100 project ideas" listicles with no author, AI-generated summaries.
- **Files provided:** none.

## Success criteria
- [ ] Answers all four research questions, each with cited sources.
- [ ] Gives at least eight concrete additional ideas, each with why it helps and how much effort it costs.
- [ ] Includes a simple way to prioritise ideas (a table or method a student can follow).
- [ ] Every fact has a citation to a source that was actually opened; gaps are disclosed.
- [ ] Readable by a beginner: plain language, no unexplained jargon.

## Constraints
- **Deadline:** same day (example run).
- **Length / effort limits:** report 1,500–2,500 words.
- **Other:** This is a demonstration of the John-vs-Jane debate pipeline; keep the debate files and the verdict.
