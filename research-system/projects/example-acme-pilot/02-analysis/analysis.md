# Analysis: Acme four-day week pilot

All data is fictional test data. Sources: [1] monthly operations data, [2] staff survey summary, [3] manager's issues log. "Before" is Jul to Dec 2025. "Pilot" is Jan to Jun 2026.

## Summary of conclusions

1. **Absence fell sharply, and this is the strongest result.** Sick days fell from 420 to 262 (-37.6%), or from 1.43 to 0.86 per head per month (-39.7%) [1]. Confidence: medium. The size and direction are clear, but there is no control group and no prior-year baseline [1].
2. **Output held up and on-time delivery improved modestly.** Total units rose 2.6% (60,870 to 62,430), but per head they were flat to slightly lower (207.0 to 205.4, -0.8%) [1]. On-time delivery averaged 94.45% against 93.12% (+1.33 points) [1]. Confidence: medium for "output was not harmed". Confidence is low for "output rose", because the rise comes from extra staff and from a weak December baseline [1].
3. **Customer complaints fell overall, but the trend reversed in June.** Complaints fell from 90 to 76 (-15.6%), and from 1.48 to 1.22 per 1,000 units [1]. June had 18, against 9 in May [1]. Confidence: medium for the six-month fall. The cause of the June spike is low confidence, because it rests on one manager's judgement [3].
4. **Wellbeing improved on average, but not for customer service.** Four of five survey measures improved. Work-life balance rose 2.9 to 4.1, burnout 2.7 to 3.6, satisfaction 3.2 to 3.9, and "would recommend" 3.4 to 4.0 [2]. "Workload is manageable" was flat (3.1 to 3.0) [2]. Customer service satisfaction was 3.1, against 4.1 for Production and 4.0 for Warehouse [2]. Confidence: medium for the overall gain. The team gap is low to medium confidence, because n=8 and there is no pre-pilot team data [2].
5. **Friday customer-service cover is the one recurring problem.** It appears in the April and June log entries [3] and in 6 survey comments, 5 of them from customer service [2]. Two sources agree, but both are internal and the log is one person's account. Confidence: medium that it is a real weakness. Confidence is low on how large it is, because there is no queue-time or Friday-specific data [EVIDENCE GAP].
6. **The evidence supports not ending the pilot. It does not yet support making it permanent without conditions.** See the options comparison and recommendation below. Confidence: medium.

## Evidence assessment

**Arithmetic re-check.** I recomputed every figure in the research notes from the raw CSV [1] and the survey [2]. These all matched: unit, on-time, complaint and sick-day totals and averages; headcount-months (294 and 304); per-head and per-1,000-unit ratios; the sensitivity check without December; and the survey weighted mean (3.86, rounds to 3.9). Response rates were 41/50 = 82% and 37/51 = 72.5% [2]. I found no discrepancies.

| Finding | Source(s) | Strength | Main limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sick days -37.6% | [1] | Moderate | Single source. Definitions not stated, and working days per month may differ. No prior-year data. |
| Sick-day pattern | [1] | Moderate | Sick days rose each month from 58 in Aug to 84 in Dec, then fell from 84 to 52 in Jan and kept falling to 38 in May. The step change at the pilot start fits an effect, but it cannot be separated from other changes. |
| Output +2.6% total, -0.8% per head | [1] | Weak to moderate | Headcount rose from 48 to 51. Hours worked are not given. |

| Complaints -15.6% | [1] | Weak to moderate | Small counts (9 to 19 a month). June ended the pilot at 18. |
| Wellbeing gains | [2] | Moderate | Summary only. Anonymous, so before and after respondents may differ. Response rates 82% vs 73%. |
| Team gap (CS 3.1) | [2][3] | Weak | n=8. June only. Team sizes not given. The log agrees, but it is not independent measurement. |

**December baseline.** December 2025 was the worst before-period month on all four operational measures (units 9,480; on-time 91.9%; complaints 19; sick days 84) [1]. Without it, the pilot still beats the baseline on all four measures, but the gaps shrink. Units are +1.2% (10,405 vs 10,278 a month). On-time is +1.09 points. Complaints are 12.67 vs 14.2 a month. Sick days are 43.67 vs 67.2 a month [1]. Per-head output is lower than this narrower baseline (about 205.4 vs 210.6, my calculation from [1]). So the safest reading is that output was maintained, not improved. The sick-day gain is large enough to survive the check.

**Confounders.** During the pilot there was also a hire in March (role not stated), meetings cut from 30 to 15 minutes, and a move to Monday to Thursday planning [1][3]. The data cannot separate these from the four-day week [EVIDENCE GAP: no control group]. The two periods cover different halves of the year [EVIDENCE GAP: no seasonality baseline].

**Patterns**
- Most gains built up through the pilot and peaked in May: on-time 95.3%, complaints 9, sick days 38 [1]. June then slipped on complaints (18) and sick days (41) [1]. One month cannot show whether this is a trend or a one-off, and no July data exists [EVIDENCE GAP].
- The staff who benefit most (work-life balance, rest and family, the most common comment with 14 mentions) are different from the staff who carry the cost (customer service, Friday cover) [2][3]. Overall averages hide this.
- "Workload is manageable" did not improve [2]. This is consistent with the same work being done in four days. It is a possible warning sign, but the 0.1 change is too small to read much into.

**Options compared with the evidence**

## Recommendations

**Recommended option: extend the pilot, with explicit conditions, with the aim of deciding on permanency at the end of the extension.** Confidence: medium. This is a judgement call, not a result in the data. The data favours keeping the four-day week. The gaps (Friday cover, short period, no baseline) argue for one more evidence round before the change becomes permanent.

The extension length is not set by the evidence. I suggest at least six months, so that the pilot can be compared with the same calendar months of the pilot's first run and with a full year [1]. The board may choose otherwise.

**Conditions**
1. **Fix Friday customer-service cover before or at the start of the extension.** Today it is one person on a rota [3]. Options include a second person on Fridays, a defined escalation route, or a plan for urgent notices. The files do not say which is best or what it would cost [EVIDENCE GAP: no cost data].
2. **Set a Friday-specific trigger plan** for events like the June recall notice, which was issued on a Thursday [3].
3. **Agree in advance the tests for moving to permanent.** Examples that follow the measures already collected: complaints stay at or below the pilot average of 12.67 a month [1]; no month reaches the 18 seen in June [1]; sick days stay well below the before-period average of 70 a month [1]; customer service satisfaction rises toward the other teams (currently 3.1 vs 4.0 to 4.1) [2]. The exact thresholds are for the board to set.
4. **Fall-back.** If Friday cover cannot be fixed or complaints keep rising, revert to ending or redesigning the pilot, not to permanent status.

**What to measure next**
- Monthly operations data from July 2026 onward, in the same format as [1], plus January to June 2025 if it exists, for a seasonality baseline.
- Complaints and response times by day of week, and phone queue times (none exist today).
- Team-level satisfaction repeated at least twice, with response rates and team sizes, so the customer service result can be tracked.
- Output and absence by team, hours worked, and definitions for on-time % and sick days.
- Turnover, cost and revenue, for a business case [EVIDENCE GAP].
- The role of the March hire, to separate the effect of extra staff.

## Assumptions and limitations

- All three sources are internal, fictional and unaudited. Reliability is medium for [1] and [2], and low to medium for [3].
- I assume the data is accurate as exported and that "before" is a fair comparison for the pilot. Neither can be tested here.
- I assume pay and the number of working hours per week did not change in ways that affect these measures. The files do not describe this [EVIDENCE GAP].
- Percentages and per-head figures were calculated from [1] and [2]. They match the researcher's, but they are not independently confirmed.
- My view that Friday cover, not the four-day week itself, drives the customer service problem comes from the log [3] and comments [2]. It is not measured.
- What would change these conclusions: (a) a similar absence drop in Jan to Jun 2025 would weaken the absence conclusion; (b) July onward showing complaints at June's level or higher would weaken the case for continuing; (c) pre-pilot team data showing customer service was already at 3.1 would mean the pilot did not cause the gap; (d) evidence that December was an anomaly would reduce the baseline gains (partly shown above).
