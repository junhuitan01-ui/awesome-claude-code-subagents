# Four-day week pilot: report to the board

## Executive summary

Acme should extend the four-day week pilot with conditions. It should not make the pilot permanent yet, and it should not end it.

Sick days fell 37.6% during the pilot, and total output and on-time delivery were maintained or slightly higher, although output per person was flat to slightly lower [1]. A four-day week has fewer working days, so part of the sick-day fall may be mechanical [1]. Staff wellbeing improved on four of five survey measures [2]. Customer service is the exception. Its staff report lower satisfaction, and Friday cover is thin [2][3].

The evidence covers six months and has no control group. That is too little for a permanent decision. We recommend an extension of at least six months, a fix for Friday cover first, and a decision test agreed in advance.

## Background

Acme Widgets Ltd ran a four-day week pilot at its single British site from January to June 2026. This report compares it with the six months before (July to December 2025). It draws on three internal files: monthly operations data [1], a staff wellbeing survey summary [2] and the operations manager's issues log [3]. All data in this report is fictional test data.

## Findings

### Output, customer service and absence

Sick days showed the largest change, but the measure may not be comparable (see Limitations). Sick days fell from 420 to 262 (-37.6%) [1]. Total units shipped rose 2.6%, but average headcount also rose, from about 49 to about 51 [1]. Output per person was therefore flat to slightly lower (-0.8%) [1]. On-time delivery averaged 94.45%, against 93.12% before [1]. Complaints fell from 90 to 76 (-15.6%) [1].

### Wellbeing

Wellbeing improved overall [2]. The score for "workload is manageable" did not improve (3.1 to 3.0) [2]. Teams differed. In June 2026, customer service scored 3.1 for satisfaction, against 4.1 for production and 4.0 for warehouse [2].

### Problems and causes

Friday customer-service cover is the one recurring problem. In April, customer service raised concerns. One person covers Fridays, phone queue times rose and no extra hire was made [3]. In June, complaints rose to 18 from nine in May [1]. Most related to slow Friday responses during a recall notice issued on a Thursday. The operations manager judged it mostly a one-off but linked it to thin Friday cover [3]. Six survey comments, five from customer service, also described stressful Friday cover [2]. In January, output dipped slightly during a two-week adjustment to Monday to Thursday planning [3].

## Limitations

The evidence shows a direction, not a final answer.

- **No control group.** Headcount rose from 50 to 51 in March (role not stated) [1]. Meetings were also shortened and planning moved to Monday to Thursday [3]. The data cannot separate these effects from the four-day week. No extra Friday-cover hire was made [3].
- **Sick-day comparability.** A four-day week has fewer working days, and the file does not define sick days. The counts may not be strictly comparable, so the fall may be partly mechanical [1].
- **Different seasons.** The two periods cover different halves of the year, and there is no earlier-year data [1].
- **A weak baseline.** December 2025 was the worst month on all four operations measures. Without it, units rose 1.2% and on-time delivery improved by 1.09 points. Sick days still fell, from 67.2 to 43.7 a month on average [1].
- **Survey limits.** We have a summary only. The survey is anonymous, so respondents may differ between surveys. Response rates were 82% in December 2025 and 73% in June 2026. The nine non-respondents in December and 14 in June may differ from respondents. The customer service result rests on eight respondents, and there is no earlier team-level survey [2].
- **Missing data.** The files give no queue times, Friday results, costs, pay, hours worked or definitions of on-time delivery and sick days, so output per hour cannot be assessed. The cause of the June rise is one manager's judgement [1][3].
- **Short period.** Six months cannot show whether June's rise in complaints is a one-off or a trend. No data exists after June [1].

## Recommendation

Extend the pilot for at least six months, with the conditions below. This is a judgement, not a result in the data. Confidence is medium. The data favours keeping the four-day week. The gaps argue for one more round of evidence before the change becomes permanent. The board may choose a different length.

**Conditions**

1. Fix Friday customer-service cover at or before the start of the extension. Options include a second person on Fridays or a defined escalation route [3]. The files give no costs.
2. Plan for urgent Friday notices, such as the June recall [3].
3. Collect better data: monthly operations data from July 2026, complaints and response times by weekday, and repeat team surveys with response rates.
4. Set a fall-back. If Friday cover cannot be fixed or complaints keep rising, redesign or end the pilot. Do not make it permanent.

**Decision test.** Move to permanent status only if, at the end of the extension:

- complaints stay at or below the pilot average of 12.67 a month [1]
- no month reaches June's 18 complaints [1]
- sick days stay well below the earlier average of 70 a month [1]
- customer service satisfaction moves towards the other teams (3.1, against 4.0 and 4.1) [2]

The board should set the exact thresholds.
