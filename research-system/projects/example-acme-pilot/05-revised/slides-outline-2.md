# Slides outline: four-day week pilot

## Slide 1. Four-day week pilot: extend with conditions
- Report to Acme's board of directors
- Pilot: January to June 2026
- Compared with July to December 2025
- All data is fictional test data

**Speaker note:** Open with the conclusion. We recommend extending the pilot, with conditions, rather than making it permanent now or ending it. All figures are fictional test data. The next slides give the evidence, the limits and the decision test.

## Slide 2. Sick days fell 37.6% and output was maintained
- Sick days: 420 down to 262 [1]
- Complaints: 90 down to 76 [1]
- On-time delivery: 93.12% up to 94.45% [1]
- Total units up 2.6%; per head down 0.8% [1]

**Speaker note:** Absence shows the largest change, but read it with care. A four-day week has fewer working days, and the file does not define sick days [1]. So the fall may be partly mechanical. Total output rose, but average headcount rose from about 49 to about 51, so output per person was flat to slightly lower [1]. Read this as "output was maintained", not "output improved". On-time delivery is an unweighted average of monthly figures. Totals, averages and percentages are our calculations from [1].

## Slide 3. Wellbeing improved overall; customer service scored lowest
- Work-life balance: 2.9 up to 4.1 [2]
- Burnout score: 2.7 up to 3.6 [2]
- Job satisfaction: 3.2 up to 3.9 [2]
- Customer service scored lowest in June: 3.1, against 4.1 and 4.0 [2]
- Workload manageable: 3.1 to 3.0, no gain [2]

**Speaker note:** Scores run from one to five, and five is best. Four of five measures improved, including "would recommend" (3.4 to 4.0) [2]. The team breakdown exists for June 2026 only, and customer service had eight respondents [2]. We cannot tell whether customer service was already lower before the pilot, or whether it improved.

## Slide 4. Friday customer-service cover is the recurring problem
- One person covers Fridays; no extra hire made [3]
- Phone queue times rose in April [3]
- June complaints reached 18, up from nine [1]
- Five of six Friday-cover comments came from customer service [2]

**Speaker note:** Most June complaints related to slow Friday responses during a recall notice issued on a Thursday [3]. The manager judged it mostly a one-off but linked it to thin Friday cover [3]. That cause is one person's judgement, and no queue-time data exists. Two internal sources agree on the weakness [2][3], so we treat it as real but cannot size it. Customer service's lower June satisfaction score may be linked, but the data cannot show this.

## Slide 5. Six months without a control group is thin evidence
- No control group; other changes happened too [1][3]
- No earlier-year or post-June data [1]
- December 2025 was weak, which flatters the comparison [1]
- Survey: summary only, response rates 82% and 73% [2]
- Sick-day definition unclear; working days may differ

**Speaker note:** Sick-day counts may not be comparable. A four-day week has fewer working days, and the file does not define the measure [1]. Excluding December, units rose 1.2% and on-time delivery gained 1.09 points [1]. Sick days still fell, from 67.2 to 43.7 a month [1]. The baseline check does not remove the working-days question. The output result is weaker. There is also no cost data and no data after June [1]. Confidence overall is medium.

## Slide 6. Extend the pilot for at least six months, with conditions
- Fix Friday customer-service cover first
- Plan for urgent Friday notices
- Collect weekday complaints, response times and team surveys
- Fall-back: redesign or end, never permanent by default
- Test: complaints at or below pilot average (12.67/month) [1]

**Speaker note:** This is a judgement, not a result in the data. Confidence is medium. The decision test for permanency also includes: no month at 18 complaints [1], sick days well below the earlier average of 70 a month [1], and customer service job satisfaction rising towards 4.0 [2]. The board sets the exact thresholds and may choose a different extension length.
